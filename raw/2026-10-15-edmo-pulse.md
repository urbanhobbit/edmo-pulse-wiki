## Ukraine strikes on Yandex and Russian trolls: facts, propaganda and doubts

**October 15, 2026** — EDMO Weekly Pulse

Ukraine strikes on Yandex and Russian trolls: facts, propaganda and doubts. ON THE RISE: A story circulating widely on social media claims that, following Ukrainian strikes on several Russian data centres belonging to Yandex, online disinformation — about the war in Ukraine and other topics — has declined significantly, along with the engagement generated by pro-Russian and far-right propaganda in Europe. The narrative, amplified by Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and supported by representatives of various governments (including the Polish government) and politicians, is highly appealing because, if confirmed, it would provide tangible evidence that the popularity of extremist anti-EU, pro-Russian forces is being artificially inflated on social media through manipulative operations directly attributable to Moscow. As of 13 October, experts and observers are still debating whether the story is true: a chart purporting to show that AfD-linked bot traffic had fallen by 56% was AI-generated, other analyses argued that monitored Russian bot activity showed no significant fluctuations, and still others found that something did happen — although the picture was more nuanced than the narrative of Russian bots disappearing after the strikes on Yandex. Because so many users reported simply noticing a difference in their everyday social-media experience, particularly on X, EDMO argues the issue cannot be dismissed out of hand as successful pro-Ukrainian propaganda, as anti-Russian disinformation or as collective autosuggestion. EDMO immediately contacted fact-checking organisations across its network: some reported sudden changes in activity patterns, such as drops in TikTok engagement for content with political hashtags typically tied to far-right and pro-Russian forces; some found significant evolution in behavioural dynamics but could not attribute causes, since the strikes may have had an impact but other explanations are possible; and others recorded no decrease in pro-Russian account activity. In short, the verdict at this stage is that there is currently no conclusive evidence that the Ukrainian attacks on Yandex data centres led to a significant decrease in activity among accounts linked to Russian propaganda and disinformation. EDMO calls on national and European agencies that hold data conclusively demonstrating the validity of the story to make it public, so that independent professional organisations can verify it — otherwise there is a risk that statements by European and national political institutions are shaped by their respective agendas — and, pending a clearer picture, urges caution and warns against letting confirmation bias prevail over rational judgement. ZOOM-IN: several EDMO network organisations investigated the possible decline of Russian bot activity after the three consecutive Ukrainian drone strikes on Yandex data centres between 8 and 11 October; while the claimed drop in pro-Kremlin traffic remains contested, all the reports acknowledge the need for further evidence gathering, and evidence continues to mount of a well-organised, multilayered influence infrastructure run by the Kremlin and its proxies across Europe, including the recruitment of YouTube influencers in Estonia, Russian cyberattacks in the Nordic countries and disinformation channelled through bogus think tanks. BROD/Funky Citizens analysed views on Romanian political hashtags on TikTok around the three drone attacks, comparing 8-11 October with 1-4 October: views fell for 54 of the 82 monitored hashtags, most sharply for #turul2inapoi (-61.2%), #suveranisti (-81.2%) and #georgesimionofficial (-95.1%), although some hashtags rose, such as #georgesimion (+51.3%) and #sos (+19.7%) — a mixed trend; Funky Citizens concludes that the attacks may have influenced the distribution of political content in Romania but that there is no robust evidence of an operational link between TikTok accounts, the analysed hashtags and Yandex infrastructure, the data indicating temporal correlations rather than a proven causal link. IBERIFIER/Maldita.es reviews the claims of disrupted Russian bot activity and reduced pro-Kremlin disinformation: experts warn against hasty conclusions, because Cloudflare Radar data shows the drop in bot-driven traffic from Yandex infrastructure is real but cannot prove a direct effect on the spread of Russian propaganda — much of the traffic trend is attributable to legitimate crawlers used by search engines to index websites — while some experts monitoring Russian influence operations detected no significant decrease in the distribution of the analysed content; on 13 October there was not sufficient evidence to claim that the Ukrainian attacks had neutralised Russian bots. NORDIS/TjekDet examined whether the strikes reduced the spread of European far-right parties, in particular the German AfD, and concluded that the evidence does not support a possible causal link: while Cloudflare data shows a strong decrease in automated traffic, bot-driven traffic includes legitimate software used by search engines to index websites and not only automated social-media accounts deployed to spread disinformation, an analysis by the Canadian company Common Consultancy could not detect significant variations in engagement with AfD or MAGA profiles on Facebook, and the provenance and methodology of the viral graphic allegedly showing a drastic drop in far-right content distribution remain unknown — hence the need for additional verifiable data and independent analyses before establishing causality. ELECTION BEAT: BECID/Re:Baltica on Latvia's 3 October election, which confirmed Prime Minister Andris Kulbergs and his United List as the clear winner with a historic 41% of seats, a record level of support for a single political force; the article highlights the rise of populist politics, the growing influence of pro-Russia voices and a campaign dominated by social-media communication, with Kulbergs adopting a direct communication style and pushing daily video blogs in a campaign centred on promises to reduce bureaucracy and public spending, while Re:Baltica fact-checkers found his statements misleading or false more frequently than those of other politicians — although, despite populist rhetoric, Latvia's pro-European foreign policy and support for Ukraine should continue. A distinctive feature of the election is the increased parliamentary representation of far-right and pro-Kremlin parties: Latvia First, led by Ainārs Šlesers, obtained 17 seats, and the pro-Russian Sovereign Power/Young Latvians Alliance, led by Jūlija Stepaņenko, gained 15, their campaigns exploiting fears surrounding immigration, vaccination, environmental policies and national security; as Re:Baltica points out, there will be many more MPs who do not shy away from misinformation or even outright disinformation and more with pro-Russian views, and the new parliament can be expected to care less about human rights, the NGO sector and media freedom. IBERIFIER/Chequeado on Spain's snap general elections, called by Pedro Sánchez for 29 November after the Spanish parliament rejected two government housing decrees amid growing public anger over the country's housing crisis, following an intense political summer marked by the Ceuta crisis; voters will elect members of both chambers of the Cortes Generales and the new parliament will then choose the next prime minister, with the campaign expected to be shaped by fierce debates over housing, economic policy and the country's wider political direction — and Sánchez already voicing concern over possible foreign interference in the campaign; Chequeado stresses that the election was triggered by a controversy that generated intense public mobilisation and political confrontation, creating even more fertile ground for misleading narratives, online manipulation and disinformation campaigns seeking to influence perceptions of the housing crisis, government performance and electoral choices in the run-up to polling day. GLOBAL PULSE: IBERIFIER on the Brazilian elections, where ahead of the presidential runoff AI-generated fake magazine covers and fabricated GloboNews broadcasts circulated widely on social media, falsely claiming that Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva had dedicated Brazil to Exu, a deity in Afro-Brazilian religions, and that First Lady Janja Lula da Silva planned to tax evangelical churches to fund Umbanda religious centres; fact-checkers found no evidence that these stories had ever been published by Veja or GloboNews, and analysis by Lupa identified the images as AI-generated, pointing to visual anomalies, recycled edition numbers from older magazine issues and inconsistencies in the manipulated content. NORDIS on the US midterm elections, where rumours spread on social media claiming that James Talarico, the Democratic candidate in Texas's closely contested Senate race, had been secretly admitted to a psychiatric facility after disappearing from the campaign trail for more than a week; Talarico rejected the allegations, explaining that he had been absent because of influenza, while the Lindner Center of Hope in Ohio publicly stated that he had never been treated there, but the claims gained traction on X, where prominent right-wing accounts circulated an unverified audio recording purporting to show a hospital employee confirming the admission and platform owner Elon Musk — having already spent a staggering $366 million supporting conservative candidates in the current campaign — personally amplified the fake narrative. IDMO on cognitive security: rather than focusing on false content itself, a new strand of research argues that the real target of modern information operations is the decision-making process, describing how uncertainty can be amplified as information moves through institutional chains, gradually transforming tentative assessments into apparently certain conclusions without the addition of new evidence; the article highlights growing interest in cognitive security among organisations such as the EU Institute for Security Studies (EUISS) and the Geneva Centre for Security Policy (GCSP), whose work suggests that countering information manipulation requires understanding the cognitive shortcuts, biases and interpretative frameworks that shape public reactions rather than focusing exclusively on the content being spread, with examples ranging from Russian military provocations to the use of artificial intelligence. ON A DIFFERENT NOTE: AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery (AIG-NCII), colloquially known as deepfake pornography, is a systemic threat to women in European politics, and the current EU framework fails these women for three reasons — it acts only after the harm is done; it depends on victims finding AIG-NCII and filing reports themselves; and its strongest duties apply only to the largest platforms, while most of the harm sits on small, often offshore sites — as set out in the report 'Targeted and Ignored. Sexualised Deepfakes of Women MEPs and the Failure of Platform Responses' by Benjamin Shultz, Stefan Heumann, Torben Klausa and Vivien Benert.
