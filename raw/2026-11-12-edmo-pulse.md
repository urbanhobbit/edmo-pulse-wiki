## The greatest threat to European information integrity is coming from the U.S. and the European far right

**November 12, 2026** — EDMO Weekly Pulse

This edition marks a shift in EDMO's threat assessment. Drawing on a French foreign ministry study and an internal EU assessment reported by Reuters on 9 November, EDMO argues that the dominant disinformation threat in Europe is no longer Russia but the de facto alliance between the American far right — now in government — and Europe's far-right forces. The evidence is quantitative: among the ten most-viewed X posts on the October riots in France (16–18 October), pro-Russian accounts drew 2.8 million views, Israeli officials, media and influencers more than 35 million, and MAGA-linked officials, media and influencers more than 49 million — almost eighteen times the Russian reach. EDMO pairs this with a methodological caveat it considers essential: opposing immigration is a legitimate opinion, but that opinion has very often been promoted through demonstrably false content, and the two must be kept analytically distinct.

### ON THE RISE

**Weekly Watch of Emerging Disinformation Risks**

On 9 November Reuters reported on an internal EU assessment describing a covert Russian network that sought to exploit last month's riots in France: pro-Russian accounts used social media to blame the unrest on uncontrolled immigration and to portray the French government as having lost control. The same article cites a French foreign ministry report finding that disinformation about the student protests was indeed spread by Russia, but to a far greater extent by the United States and Israel. The French study measured the ten most-viewed X posts on the unrest between 16 and 18 October:

- **Pro-Russian spheres:** 2.8 million views.
- **Israeli officials, government accounts, media outlets and influencers:** more than 35 million views.
- **MAGA-linked officials, media outlets and influencers:** more than 49 million views — almost 18 times the Russian figure.

EDMO reads this as consistent with its own observations in previous Weekly Pulse editions and in the introduction to the latest Monthly Report: the United States — through the Trump administration and the MAGA movement — and the European far right have most widely disseminated the narrative that the French protests are not about students' grievances but a violent uprising by Muslim immigrants who should be "remigrated" to prevent the "Great Replacement" of European populations. Russia played the same tune through known operations such as *Matryoshka*, *Storm-1516* and *Overload*, but their impact was limited.

**Why it matters for 2027.** Looking at the EU elections due in 2027, starting with France itself, EDMO concludes that the main disinformation threat in Europe does not appear to be Russia, which played a minor role in these crises (unlike in disinformation about the war in Ukraine), but rather the de facto alliance between the American far right in government and Europe's far-right forces. The dynamic is not new: it was already visible in the August 2026 Ceuta migration crisis.

**The conceptual distinction EDMO insists on.** The relationship between political views and disinformation is highly sensitive, so EDMO restates its pre-2024 methodology: a "disinformation narrative" is a clear message emerging from a consistent set of contents that can be demonstrated as false using fact-checking methodology, and such a narrative may overlap with a legitimate opinion — but the two must be kept distinct. Arguing that there are too many immigrants, particularly Muslim immigrants, and that their numbers should be reduced, is a legitimate opinion; running campaigns to convey that view is legitimate too. What is undeniable is that this opinion has very often been promoted through demonstrably false content: migrants portrayed as violent, as destroying Christian symbols, as parasites, as Islamic terrorists, as eating pets, as spreading diseases, as replacing Europeans. Disinformation about France is "just the latest brick in an already towering wall". Equally undeniable is that these narratives are increasingly spread not only by European far-right parties, which derive direct electoral benefit, but also by foreign states acting for domestic and foreign policy reasons — and among them stands out the United States, at both administration and MAGA-movement level.

### ZOOM-IN

**A Closer Look at Cases Detected by the EDMO Network** — three cases showing how domestic far-right movements capitalise on foreign interference.

As EDMO's October 2026 Overview pointed out, there is insufficient evidence to attribute the protests in France, Italy or elsewhere to a single Russian, Islamist or other source. Foreign influence operations are a real risk and pro-Russian networks are actively exploiting European social tensions through an increasingly sophisticated infrastructure, but other foreign actors also rally on the grievances expressed by student protests. Whatever the origin of the interference, domestic far-right movements are now capitalising on it to spread xenophobic narratives and turn social problems into identity conflicts.

**The UN didn't publish plans to replace Irish people in Ireland or Europeans in Europe** *(from EDMO Ireland)*
A widely shared video claims the United Nations set out a plan to replace the Irish and European population through immigration, referring to the Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration (the Marrakech Pact), approved in 2018 with Irish participation. *The Journal*'s analysis of the official document finds no demographic replacement plan — the word "Europe" does not even appear in the text. The Pact sets 23 objectives for safe and orderly migration management, including data collection, prevention of irregular border crossings and human trafficking. The article links the false claim to the "Great Replacement" conspiracy theory routinely promoted in racist online circles.

**"Five churches burned down in one day by Muslims"? A figure invented by Islamophobic accounts** *(from DE FACTO)*
*De Facto* disproved the claim that Muslims set fire to five churches in France in a single day. The story spread mainly through Islamophobic and anti-immigrant accounts, including American conspiracy podcaster Alex Jones and MAGA Nation 47, and rests on decontextualised images: a video captioned as a church fire actually shows a burning dumpster in Lyon; another image depicts the 2019 fire at Saint-Sulpice in Paris. According to the Observatory for Religious Heritage, only one fire has been reported since 17 October — Saint-Médard in Fenouillet near Toulouse, of unknown origin. The article acknowledges vandalism and a rise in attacks on Christian heritage while warning against manipulation aimed at fuelling anti-Islamic sentiment.

**Video shows Easter bonfires in Cyprus instead of riots in Berlin** *(from GADMO)*
A video purporting to document violent confrontations during the late-October demonstrations in Berlin reached more than a million views; Herbert Kickl, leader of the Austrian FPÖ, also used it to illustrate the German protests. GADMO's verification shows the footage records a traditional Easter bonfire held in May 2024 in Limassol, Cyprus, near the church of St. Nicholas — established by locating earlier TikTok posts and comparing the building with Google Street View imagery.

### ELECTION BEAT

**Tracking electoral disinformation through EDMO Hubs** — Spain, three weeks out from the 29 November vote.

**Hoaxes, disinformation and context surrounding Spain's 29 November 2026 general election** *(from IBERIFIER)*
*Maldita.es* has identified an increase in disinformation targeting electoral integrity and political actors. A recurrent theme is unfounded voter-fraud claims, mainly around mail voting: AI-generated videos on TikTok, Instagram, Facebook and X falsely show post office workers destroying ballots and manipulating the process, reinforcing "rigged election" narratives without evidence. Fact-checkers have also debunked manipulated images, false polls, AI-generated content and recycled videos presented as current. Several viral cases cast migrants, especially Muslim groups, as influencing the election, while others misuse old footage, alter photographs or misrepresent candidates' statements — all of it raising distrust and polarisation before election day.

**Disinformation network Pravda replicates Spanish far-right Telegram posts about upcoming general elections** *(from IBERIFIER)*
An investigation by IBERIFIER partner *Newtral.es* finds that Pravda, the Russian-linked disinformation network, has republished content from Spanish far-right and conspiratorial Telegram channels during the campaign. Analysing more than 68,000 Pravda headlines and 667,000 Telegram posts, the study shows many Spain-focused Pravda articles appeared minutes after being posted on Spanish channels; 86% of replicated headlines originated from far-right and conspiratorial sources such as España Eterna, Viejos Tercios and El Chico Triste, and election-related content plus vote-fraud narratives surged once the election was called. The finding matters analytically: Pravda acts less as an originator of narratives than as an amplifier, redistributing misinformation that originates domestically.

### GLOBAL PULSE

**Disinformation narratives shaping the world's conversations** — the US midterms, post-mortem.

**Fact-checkers flag widespread misinformation in record-level US election advertising** *(from NORDIS)*
Following the 2026 US midterms, NORDIS partner *TjekDet* reviewed how unprecedented campaign spending was accompanied by widespread misinformation. According to AdImpact, around $3 billion was spent on Senate election advertising alone, with the Texas Senate race becoming the most expensive non-presidential campaign in US history. Much of the funding came from outside interest groups, including Super PACs, which poured vast sums into television, radio and online advertising. Misleading and false claims featured prominently in ads across key battleground states; drawing on fact-checks from organisations such as PolitiFact, the article documents how deceptive political messaging is amplified through heavily funded advertising.

**Viral Texas ballot-fraud narrative collapses under scrutiny** *(from GADMO)*
A GADMO and AFP fact-check debunks viral claims that the FBI uncovered 20,000 fraudulent ballots for Democratic candidates during a raid in Houston ahead of the midterms. The FBI confirmed no such operation took place, and the images used to support the claim were unrelated and out of context. The story originated on social media and was amplified across platforms. It follows an earlier fact-check on a similar Houston claim: details varied but both centred on the same unfounded fraud allegation and misleading visual evidence, showing how recycled ballot-manipulation narratives persist despite clear law-enforcement denials.

**How "pink slime" websites and AI content influenced the US midterms** *(from IDMO)*
*Facta* examines how both major political camps used increasingly sophisticated information tactics. It focuses on "pink slime" outlets — partisan sites designed to resemble local independent newspapers, with names such as "Ohio Pulse" or "Iowa Voice", promoting content favourable to specific candidates — and cites NewsGuard research finding that both Democratic- and Republican-linked organisations funded such networks and amplified their content through paid social campaigns. The article also documents "slopaganda": AI-generated videos and social content used to attack opponents and shape narratives at scale, reaching millions of users on Facebook, Instagram and TikTok and blurring the line between political advertising, influence campaigns and journalism.
